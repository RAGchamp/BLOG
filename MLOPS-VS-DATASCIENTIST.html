0. The Partnership in One Picture

A model that only lives in a notebook has delivered zero business value. A pipeline that runs reliably but serves a bad model has also delivered zero value. Neither role can produce the outcome alone — the Data Scientist is accountable for whether the model is right; the MLOps Engineer is accountable for whether the model runs, reliably, at scale, safely, and stays that way.

1. Two Roles, One Lifecycle — Overview

Think of it as research vs. engineering applied to the same artifact: the Data Scientist runs the scientific process that produces a model worth shipping; the MLOps Engineer runs the software/infrastructure process that turns that model into a reliable, observable, governed production service. Neither is "more senior" — they are different disciplines with different failure modes, and a mature ML org treats the boundary between them as a well-defined, tooled handoff rather than a wall.

1.1 — Why the split exists
  • A model is two things bolted together: a statistical artifact (weights, a decision boundary, a set of learned parameters) and a piece of software that must run under load, fail gracefully, and be observable. Optimizing for one without the other is how "it worked in the notebook" becomes an incident.
  • The skills barely overlap: DS needs statistics, domain knowledge, and experimentation discipline; MLOps needs distributed systems, CI/CD, Kubernetes/cloud infra, and SRE-style operational rigor. Very few people are excellent at both at scale.
  • The split also creates accountability: when accuracy degrades, that's a DS problem to diagnose; when the endpoint is down or slow, that's an MLOps problem to fix — without a split, "the model is broken" becomes nobody's clearly owned page.
1.2 — The four phases this doc uses

OperationalizeCan this model be packaged, deployed, and served reliably at the required scale/latency?MLOps Engineer (DS validates behavior)
MaintainDoes the model keep working as data, code, and the world change?MLOps Engineer runs it; Data Scientist decides what "working" means

2. The Data Scientist — Role & Responsibilities

The Data Scientist is accountable for whether the model is correct and valuable — translating a business question into a measurable ML problem, and finding/validating the best solution to it.

2.1 — Core responsibilities
  • Problem framing: convert a vague business ask ("reduce churn") into a well-posed ML problem (a target label, a time horizon, a success metric, and a decision the prediction will drive).
  • Data exploration & quality assessment: understand the available data, its coverage, biases, leakage risks, and gaps before any modeling starts.
  • Feature engineering: design and validate the signals the model will learn from, including their business meaning and stability over time.
  • Model selection & training: choose candidate algorithms/architectures, tune hyperparameters, and train under a disciplined experiment-tracking process.
  • Offline evaluation: hold-out and cross-validation metrics, error analysis by segment, fairness/bias checks, and comparison against a baseline (including "no model" / current heuristic).
  • Statistical rigor: confidence intervals, significance testing for A/B results, and honesty about uncertainty — not just a single headline metric.
  • Business translation: explain what the model does and doesn't do, in terms stakeholders can act on, and set expectations about accuracy vs. cost/latency trade-offs.
  • Ongoing model quality ownership: defines what "still good" means (the metric and its threshold) so monitoring and retraining have a target to check against.
2.2 — What a Data Scientist is not expected to own
  • Provisioning cloud infrastructure, Kubernetes clusters, or networking.
  • Writing production-grade serving code, load balancing, or autoscaling policy.
  • Building and maintaining the CI/CD pipeline that ships the model.
  • 24/7 on-call for the serving endpoint being up or down.
  • Security hardening of the runtime environment, secrets management, or network policy.

In smaller teams a DS may do some of this out of necessity — but as the org matures, these move to MLOps so DS time goes back into modeling.

3. The MLOps Engineer — Role & Responsibilities

The MLOps Engineer is accountable for whether the model runs — reliably, reproducibly, at the required scale, with the guardrails a production system needs, for as long as it stays in production.

3.1 — Core responsibilities
  • Platform & tooling: stand up and maintain the experiment-tracking system (MLflow/W&B), the feature store, the model registry, and shared compute (GPU/CPU clusters, notebooks-as-a-service) that Data Scientists build on.
  • Reproducible pipelines: turn a notebook or training script into a versioned, parameterized, re-runnable pipeline (data → features → train → evaluate → register), typically as a DAG (Airflow/Kubeflow/Azure ML pipelines/SageMaker Pipelines).
  • Packaging & deployment: containerize the model and its serving code, define the serving pattern (real-time API, batch job, streaming), and deploy it via CI/CD with proper versioning and rollback.
  • Infrastructure as code: provision and manage the compute, networking, storage, and identity the ML system runs on, following the same IaC/GitOps discipline as any other production service.
  • CI/CD for ML: automated testing (schema, unit, integration), automated retraining triggers, model registry promotion gates, canary/shadow/blue-green rollout strategies for new model versions.
  • Observability: instrument latency, throughput, error rate, resource utilization, and data/prediction pipelines for drift signals; build the dashboards and alerts on top of them.
  • Reliability & on-call: SLOs for the serving endpoint, incident response, capacity planning, disaster recovery, and rollback procedures.
  • Governance & security: access control to models/data, secrets management, audit trails of what model version served which prediction, and compliance requirements (data residency, PII handling, model cards/documentation).
  • Cost management: right-sizing training and inference infrastructure, autoscaling policy, and spend visibility per model/team.
3.2 — What an MLOps Engineer is not expected to own
  • Choosing the modeling approach, feature set, or hyperparameters.
  • Deciding whether an accuracy/precision/recall number is "good enough" for the business.
  • Interpreting model errors in domain terms (why the model is wrong on this segment).
  • Statistical design of experiments/A-B tests (though they build the infra to run them).

MLOps can flag that a metric moved (via monitoring); only DS can say whether that matters and what to do about it.

5. Phase 1 — Build

Goal: produce a model that is demonstrably good enough to be worth operationalizing. This phase is DS-led, running on a platform MLOps provides and maintains.

5.1 — What the Data Scientist does
  1. Frame the problem: target variable, prediction horizon, decision the model informs, and the metric that will define success (e.g. AUC, MAE, precision@k) tied to a business KPI.
  2. Explore the data: distributions, missingness, label leakage, class imbalance, temporal effects.
  3. Engineer and validate features; check for train/serve skew risk early (a feature computable offline but not at inference time is a dead end).
  4. Train multiple candidate models, tracking every run (params, data version, code version, metrics) in the experiment tracker.
  5. Evaluate offline: hold-out performance, segment-level error analysis, fairness/bias checks, comparison to the current baseline/heuristic.
  6. Package the winning approach as a clear, reproducible training recipe (script or notebook with pinned dependencies and data references) and hand it off with an evaluation report.
5.2 — What the MLOps Engineer provides
  • Self-service, governed access to training data and compute (notebooks, GPU pools, Spark/Databricks clusters).
  • An experiment-tracking system (MLflow, Weights & Biases) so every run is comparable and auditable.
  • A feature store so features are computed once, consistently, for both training and serving — the single biggest lever against train/serve skew.
  • Data versioning (DVC, Delta Lake, lakeFS) so a training run can always be traced back to the exact data it used.
  • Guardrails: environment reproducibility (pinned images/lockfiles), so "works on my machine" doesn't become "works nowhere else."

Hand-off gate: a model only leaves Build when it has a registered version, a documented evaluation report, known input/output schema, and a named DS owner who signs off on "this is good enough to ship."

6. Phase 2 — Operationalize

Goal: turn the validated model into a running production service. This phase is MLOps-led; the Data Scientist stays involved to validate that production behavior matches offline expectations.

6.1 — What the MLOps Engineer does
  1. Pull the model artifact from the registry and wrap it in serving code (a REST/gRPC endpoint, a batch job, or a streaming consumer, depending on the use case's latency needs).
  2. Containerize it, following the same supply-chain discipline as any production image: pinned base image, vulnerability scan, SBOM, signed and built only in CI.
  3. Choose and implement the serving pattern:
    • Real-time/online: low-latency API behind autoscaling (Kubernetes + HPA, or a managed endpoint like Azure ML/SageMaker/Vertex AI endpoints).
    • Batch: scheduled scoring job writing predictions to a table/warehouse.
    • Streaming: a consumer scoring events off a queue/topic (Kafka/Event Hubs) in near-real-time.
  4. Build the CI/CD pipeline: automated tests (schema validation, a small "sanity" prediction set, load test) gate promotion from staging to production.
  5. Roll out safely: shadow mode (score in parallel, don't act on it) → canary (a small % of traffic) → full rollout, with an automatic or one-command rollback path.
  6. Wire the model version, input schema, and prediction logs into the same observability stack as the rest of production (structured logs, traces, metrics).
6.2 — What the Data Scientist validates
  • Confirms that predictions coming out of the deployed service match what was expected offline (a small golden-set parity check — same inputs, same outputs, within tolerance).
  • Signs off on the shadow/canary results before full rollout: are live predictions distributed as expected, and does the online metric (if measurable in near-real-time) track the offline one?
  • Defines the acceptance thresholds that the CI/CD pipeline's automated tests check against.

Common failure here: training/serving skew — the features computed in the training pipeline (often in Python/pandas) don't exactly match the features computed in the low-latency serving path (often reimplemented in a different language or system). A shared feature store closes this gap; anything else needs an explicit parity test in the pipeline.

7. Phase 3 — Maintain

Goal: keep the system running and the model correct as data, upstream systems, and the world change. A model is never "done" — it decays. This phase is run by MLOps, on a cadence and against thresholds that the Data Scientist defines.

7.1 — Why models need maintenance at all
  • Data drift: the statistical distribution of input features shifts over time (new user segments, seasonality, a change in an upstream system) even if the relationship between features and label hasn't.
  • Concept drift: the relationship between inputs and the true label changes (customer behavior evolves, a competitor changes the market, a fraud pattern adapts).
  • Upstream data pipeline changes: a schema change, a silently broken ETL job, a unit change (cents → dollars) — these break models without any code change to the model itself.
  • Software/dependency changes: library upgrades, infrastructure migrations, or a change to a feature's computation logic.
7.2 — What the MLOps Engineer runs
  • Automated or scheduled retraining pipelines (triggered on a cadence, on a drift alert, or on demand), re-running the DS-authored training recipe against fresh data.
  • Model registry lifecycle: staging → production → archived, with every version retained and traceable to its training data and code.
  • Rollback procedures: a one-command revert to the previous production model version if the new one underperforms.
  • Dependency and infrastructure upkeep: base image patches, Kubernetes/cluster upgrades, library CVE remediation — without breaking model behavior.
  • Champion/challenger and shadow evaluation infrastructure so a new candidate model can be compared against the current one on live traffic before promotion.
7.3 — What the Data Scientist decides
  • Sets the retraining trigger conditions in business/statistical terms (e.g. "retrain if PSI on top-5 features > 0.2" or "retrain if 7-day rolling accuracy drops > 3 points").
  • Re-validates every retrained candidate offline before it's promoted — retraining is not automatically re-approving.
  • Decides whether a drift alert requires a full retrain, a feature fix, or is actually a benign, expected shift (not every drift is a problem).
  • Owns the decision to deprecate a model entirely when it's no longer worth maintaining.

8. Phase 4 — Monitor

Goal: know, continuously and without manual investigation, whether the system is healthy and the model is still good. Monitoring spans two layers that require different skills and different owners.

8.2 — Model/statistical monitoring (built by MLOps, interpreted by DS)

MLOps builds and operates the pipelines that compute these (often via a dedicated ML monitoring tool: Evidently, WhyLabs, Arize, Fiddler, or a cloud-native equivalent) and wires the alerts. The Data Scientist defines the thresholds and is the one paged (or looped in) when a statistical/quality alert fires — system alerts (endpoint down) page MLOps.

A useful rule of thumb: "is it up and fast?" is an MLOps question; "is it right?" is a Data Scientist question. Good monitoring answers both, routed to the right owner automatically.

9. Automation, Testing & the Champion/Challenger Feedback Loop

This is the mechanism that turns "monitor" and "maintain" into a continuous, low-drama cycle instead of a fire drill: production predictions become tomorrow's evaluation data, new candidate models are compared against the live model automatically, and promotion is a tested, reversible pipeline step — not a manual redeploy. MLOps builds and operates this infrastructure; the Data Scientist is its primary user, defining what "better" means and reading its output.

9.1 — The feedback loop, end to end

Every production prediction is an opportunity to learn whether the model is still right — but only if it is captured, joined with the outcome once known, and fed back into evaluation and training. That closed loop is infrastructure, not a one-off script.

The Data Scientist treats this loop as a self-service tool: register a challenger, define the comparison metric, read the verdict. They never need to touch the logging pipeline, the label-join job, or the traffic-splitting logic underneath it — that plumbing is MLOps's to build once and keep running for every model that uses it.

9.2 — Champion vs. Challenger, in detail

Champion is the model currently serving production traffic and making the real decisions. Challenger is a candidate replacement — a retrained version, a new architecture, a different feature set — that has already passed offline evaluation and now needs to prove itself against reality before it's trusted with real decisions.

9.3 — Automated testing across the ML pipeline

"Tests" for an ML system cover more ground than for typical software, because both the code and the data can be wrong. MLOps builds and maintains all of these as pipeline/CI gates; the Data Scientist defines what each test should check.

Test typeWhat it catchesTypical tooling
Data validation / schema testsMissing columns, type changes, out-of-range values, null spikes in a new data batchGreat Expectations, Pandera, Deequ

The through-line: every one of these is written once, by MLOps, as a reusable pipeline stage — and reused for every model and every retrain, so "did we test this?" is answered by the pipeline having run, not by asking someone to remember.

The pattern to internalize: the Data Scientist decides what counts as better and what counts as broken; the MLOps Engineer builds the always-on machinery — logging, label joins, traffic splitting, statistical comparison, CI gates — that answers those questions automatically, every time, for every model, without either side re-inventing it per project.

11. Platform Deep Dive — Databricks & Snowflake

Most real-world ML orgs run on top of one (or both) of these platforms rather than assembling every piece from scratch. Both blur the DS/MLOps line in useful ways — each gives Data Scientists a self-service surface (notebooks, SQL, Python) while MLOps owns the governance, orchestration, and cost/perf tuning underneath it.

11.1 — Databricks: Unity Catalog & ML orchestration
11.1.1  Unity Catalog — the governance layer

Unity Catalog is Databricks' unified governance layer for all data and AI assets — not just tables. It gives the lakehouse a single, three-level namespace (catalog.schema.object) spanning tables, views, files (Volumes), functions, and — critically for ML — models and features, so a Data Scientist queries and registers assets the same way regardless of whether the underlying thing is a Delta table or a model version.

  • Unified namespace for ML assets: the MLflow Model Registry lives inside Unity Catalog (catalog.schema.model_name), so a model version has the same lineage, access control, and discoverability as the table it was trained on — one governance boundary for data and models.
  • Fine-grained access control: table-, row-, and column-level security (including dynamic views and column masking for PII) enforced centrally, so a Data Scientist's notebook and a production scoring job hit the exact same permission checks.
  • End-to-end lineage: Unity Catalog automatically tracks lineage from raw table → feature → training run → registered model → serving endpoint, so "what data trained the model that's live right now" is a lineage-graph lookup, not an investigation.
  • Feature & Function Serving: the Databricks Feature Store is Unity-Catalog-native — features are just governed Delta tables with declared primary/lookup keys, usable for both training (point-in-time correct joins) and low-latency online serving.
  • Audit logging: every read/write/query against a governed asset is logged centrally, which is what makes SOC2/HIPAA-style compliance tractable for ML pipelines touching sensitive data.
11.1.2  Orchestration for ML on Databricks
  • Databricks Workflows (Jobs): the native orchestrator for multi-task pipelines — a typical ML job graph is ingest → feature engineering → train → evaluate → register → deploy, each task a notebook, script, or SQL query, with retries, alerting, and a schedule or trigger (e.g. "run when new data lands").
  • Delta Live Tables (DLT): declarative pipelines for the ingestion/feature side — you declare the transformation logic and DLT manages incremental processing, data-quality expectations, and dependency ordering, feeding curated Delta tables that training jobs consume.
  • MLflow (native, Unity-Catalog-backed): experiment tracking, model packaging, and the model registry are built in — a training run's params/metrics/artifacts and the resulting registered model version all live in the same governed catalog.
  • Model Serving: a registered Unity Catalog model can be deployed to a managed, autoscaling REST endpoint (or a batch/streaming job) directly from the registry — the "operationalize" phase (§6) becomes a few clicks/API calls on top of a model that's already governed.
  • Databricks Asset Bundles (DABs): infra-as-code for Databricks itself — jobs, pipelines, and model-serving endpoints are defined as YAML and deployed via CI/CD, so MLOps manages the whole ML workflow the same way it manages Terraform/Bicep elsewhere.
  • Lakehouse Monitoring: built on Unity Catalog, auto-generates data/feature drift and model-quality dashboards on top of the same governed tables the model trains from — directly feeding the feedback loop in §9.

11.2 — Snowflake: data warehousing capabilities for ML
11.2.1  The core warehousing model

Snowflake's foundational property is the separation of storage and compute: data sits once in Snowflake's managed storage layer while any number of independently sized virtual warehouses (compute clusters) can query it concurrently without contending for resources. For ML workloads this means a Data Scientist's heavy feature-engineering query never slows down a production dashboard or a real-time scoring job — they simply run on different warehouses against the same governed data.

  • Elastic, workload-isolated compute: spin up a warehouse sized (X-Small to 6X-Large) for a specific job — a small one for ad-hoc DS exploration, a large one for a nightly feature-recompute job — and suspend it when idle (pay-per-second billing).
  • Zero-copy cloning: instantly clone an entire database/schema/table (metadata-only, no data copy) to snapshot a training dataset or spin up an isolated dev/test environment — the basis for reproducible "what data trained this model" snapshots.
  • Time Travel & Fail-safe: query data as it existed at a prior point in time (up to 90 days), which lets a Data Scientist reconstruct the exact feature values that existed when a historical prediction was made — essential for debugging drift or an audit.
  • Semi-structured data support: native VARIANT columns for JSON/Avro/Parquet mean raw event or API data can be loaded and queried without a rigid upfront schema, common for feature-rich ML use cases.
  • Data Sharing / Marketplace: securely share live (not copied) data across accounts/orgs — a common pattern for bringing in third-party enrichment data for feature engineering without building an ingestion pipeline.
  • Governance: role-based access control, row-access policies, dynamic data masking, and object tagging give MLOps the same fine-grained control over training data access as Unity Catalog does on Databricks.
11.2.2  Snowflake's ML-specific extensions
  • Snowpark (Python/Java/Scala): lets a Data Scientist write DataFrame-style feature engineering and even train models inside Snowflake's compute, avoiding an ETL hop to move data out to a separate Spark/pandas environment — the data never leaves the governed warehouse.
  • Snowpark ML: a modeling API (preprocessing, scikit-learn-compatible model training, hyperparameter tuning) that runs distributed on warehouse compute, plus a Model Registry for versioning and deploying models as warehouse-native functions.
  • Snowflake Feature Store: declares and manages features directly on top of warehouse tables, with point-in-time-correct retrieval for training and low-latency lookups for serving — the warehouse equivalent of Databricks' Feature Store.
  • Cortex: Snowflake-managed LLM and ML functions (summarization, sentiment, forecasting, anomaly detection) callable directly in SQL, useful for lightweight ML tasks that don't warrant a custom model.
  • Snowpark Container Services: runs arbitrary containerized workloads (including custom model-serving containers or GPU training jobs) inside Snowflake's security perimeter, closing the gap between "data warehouse" and "ML platform."
  • Tasks & Streams: Snowflake's native scheduling (Tasks) and change-data-capture (Streams) primitives can drive simple feature-refresh or retraining-trigger pipelines entirely in SQL, without an external orchestrator for lighter-weight cases.

Databricks and Snowflake increasingly overlap (Snowflake added Snowpark/Container Services for compute-heavy ML; Databricks added Lakehouse Federation and stronger SQL/warehousing ergonomics). The practical dividing line for most orgs: Databricks when the workload is compute-heavy, notebook-first ML/AI engineering; Snowflake when the workload is warehouse-centric analytics with ML layered on top of already-governed tables. Many orgs run both, connected via Lakehouse Federation or straightforward data sharing.

15. A Day in the Life — Example Incident

A worked example showing the two roles in motion together: an alert fires that a fraud-detection model's precision has dropped.

15.1 — Timeline
  1. 08:14 — The ML monitoring platform (owned by MLOps) fires an alert: 7-day rolling precision on the fraud model dropped from 0.91 to 0.78. It pages the on-call Data Scientist and posts to a shared channel.
  2. 08:20 — MLOps first rules out an infrastructure cause: checks the feature pipeline's job logs, confirms all upstream data landed on time and the serving endpoint's latency/error rate are nominal. Infra is healthy.
  3. 08:35 — The Data Scientist pulls the drift dashboard (MLOps-built, DS-read): one feature — "transaction velocity in the last hour" — shows a large PSI spike starting three days ago.
  4. 08:50 — Joint triage: MLOps traces the spike to an upstream payments team change (a new transaction type was added). It's not model decay — it's a definition change the model was never trained on.
  5. 09:15 — The Data Scientist decides: retrain with the new transaction type included, rather than trying to filter it out. Updates the feature spec.
  6. 09:30 — MLOps triggers the retraining pipeline against fresh data; it runs unattended, registers a new candidate model version, and runs the automated offline test suite.
  7. 11:00 — The Data Scientist reviews the new candidate's offline evaluation: precision recovers to 0.93. Signs off.
  8. 11:15 — MLOps promotes the new version through the CI/CD pipeline: shadow mode for 2 hours, then a 10% canary.
  9. 13:30 — Canary metrics look healthy on both operational (latency, errors) and statistical (precision, prediction distribution) dashboards. MLOps completes the rollout to 100%.
  10. 13:35 — Both write a short postmortem: root cause (upstream schema change untracked by data contracts), fix (retrain + updated feature), and a follow-up action item (add a data contract / schema alert with the payments team so this is caught before it degrades the model next time).

Notice what made this fast: a monitoring system that surfaced the right layer of signal (feature drift, not just "endpoint is fine"), a registry and CI/CD pipeline that made retrain-and-redeploy a known, repeatable, low-risk motion, and a clear rule for who decides what.

16. Checklist for a Healthy DS/MLOps Handoff

16.1 — Before a model ships
  • Model is registered with a version, its training data/code lineage, and a written evaluation report.
  • Input/output schema is explicit and validated at the serving boundary.
  • Features used in training exist in the feature store's online path with proven parity.
  • Success metric and its retraining/rollback thresholds are written down, not tribal knowledge.
  • A named DS owner and a named MLOps owner both exist for this model.
16.2 — Before a model goes fully live
  • Shadow or canary results reviewed and signed off by DS.
  • Rollback path tested, not just documented.
  • Both system-health and model-quality dashboards exist, with alert routing to the correct owner.
  • Model card / documentation published (purpose, limitations, training data summary, known biases).
16.3 — Ongoing
  • Drift and quality dashboards are actually reviewed on a cadence, not just built and forgotten.
  • Every retrained/promoted model version goes through the same offline re-validation as the original.
  • Incident postmortems feed back into either the monitoring thresholds, the feature set, or the pipeline — not just a Slack thread that gets lost.
  • On-call rotation matches ownership: MLOps for the service being up, DS for the model being right.
